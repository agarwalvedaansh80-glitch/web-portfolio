Case study · landing page conversion

One page, one job: get a café owner to reserve a seat.

A 6-week live course that teaches independent café owners to run their own Instagram in 20 minutes a day, for $249. The first pass made the page clear. This pass gives it an identity that matches the product: a café specials board, built as a bento grid with chalk type that writes itself on, and every interaction tied to a doubt it removes.

The problem

The original page looked tidy but never said what the course was, who it was for, what it cost or when it started. The only action was an eight-field enquiry form at the very bottom.

The approach

Answer the five questions a busy owner has (what, for me, how long, how much, what if it doesn’t work) in the first screen, then handle each remaining doubt in the order it comes up, and let the visitor try the promise instead of just reading it.

The result

Offer, price, start date, deadline and refund in the first screen at 1440px and 390px wide, one primary action repeated at each decision point, and a page that feels like a café rather than a template.

A note on numbers. The redesigned page has not yet run with live traffic, so no conversion figures are claimed and no testimonials appear until real ones exist. The phone’s likes and door counter are labelled as an illustration. Expected effects are described in plain terms, and the test plan below is how they would be proved.

The first screen, side by side

Most visitors decide whether to keep reading from what they see before scrolling, so this is where most of the work went.

What changed and why

Each change is tied to the doubt it removes.

Headline

Clear value proposition. Name the audience, the outcome and the effort in one line so the right visitor recognises themselves at once.

Before

“Unlock Your Digital Potential.” True of any product.

After

“Run your café’s Instagram in 20 minutes a day,” chalked on the board.

Primary action

One goal, visible early. A single primary button that says what happens, repeated at each decision point and nowhere else.

Before

“Learn more” scrolls down; the real action is “Submit” under a form at the bottom.

After

“Reserve my seat” in the hero, the planner, the curriculum, pricing, the closing band and the sticky phone bar. Same words, same tomato red, the only red button on the page.

Price

Cost transparency. A hidden price is a reason to leave and a reason to distrust.

Before

“Pricing details are shared upon enquiry.”

After

$249 on the hero button and on its own tile, with the other two tiers named in one line. Three cards later, the live cohort set apart as the default.

Form friction

Fewer steps to commit. Ask only for what the next step needs.

Before

Eight fields, six required, to receive a quote.

After

No form. The button goes straight to checkout, email first, no account.

Value before price

Show the size of the offer before the price table, so $249 is read against what it buys.

Before

Three generic “Why choose us” icons.

After

A bento grid of nine concrete things you get, each tile showing its item working: the live call, the feed review, the calendar of scheduled posts.

Curriculum

Outcomes over features. Owners buy what they will have at the end of each week.

Before

“Introduction to Social Media Ecosystems and Brand Identity Frameworks.”

After

Six week panels, each ending in “You leave with …” and a concrete deliverable, and a seventh panel with the button.

Who it’s for, and not for

Self-qualification. Saying who should not buy makes the rest of the page more believable and cuts refunds.

Before

“Businesses of all sizes.”

After

Two short lists, including “you want thousands of followers fast”.

Risk reversal

Lower the cost of being wrong.

Before

Nothing.

After

A 30-day refund stamp in the hero, in the bento grid and beside pricing, plus a free move to the next cohort.

Deadline

Real, not manufactured, urgency. The countdown follows an actual rule and never resets on reload.

Before

No dates at all.

After

Enrolment closes the Sunday before each monthly start at 23:59. Shown as a hero tile, a flip clock in the closing band and a line in the sticky bar.

Objections

Answer doubts where they arise.

Before

No FAQ. Questions meant a form and a wait.

After

Eight questions, including an honest “we can’t promise a number,” and the biggest one (“is 20 minutes realistic?”) turned into a planner you can try.

Navigation

Fewer exits. A landing page has one job.

Before

Seven links, including Blog and Resources, plus four footer links.

After

Four in-page links and the primary button. Hidden on phones, where the sticky bar takes over.

Social proof

Only real proof counts. Invented reviews are worse than none.

Before

“Expert instructors. Proven strategies. Real results.”

After

A dashed, labelled slot reserved for real student results after the first cohort.

The interactive layer, and what each part is for

The rule for this pass: motion only where it shows the promise or lowers a doubt. The button and price are readable from the first frame, nothing waits for an animation to finish, and every effect has a calm static version.

Hero

Chalk headline that writes itself

Each letter is drawn as a chalk stroke, then filled, in about two seconds, and nudges away from the pointer afterwards. It pulls the eye to the promise first. The fact tiles and button are already on screen while it writes.

Hero

Specials roller

“You leave with …” rolls through five outcomes letter by letter, like a board being changed. Five reasons to buy in one line, without five lines of copy.

Hero

Bento fact tiles

Price, start date, a ticking countdown and the refund stamp, one tile each. A tile is one question answered, so the first screen reads in the order owners ask.

Scroll story

A phone that fills post by post

The section pins and scrubs with the scroll: twelve café posts drawn on canvas pop into a feed, likes count up, and a door counter rolls from 212 to 341. It shows the end state, a feed that keeps itself going and visits you can count, and it is labelled as an illustration.

Value

Bento grid with a job per tile

Tiles tilt toward the pointer and each shows its item working: a live waveform, a red pen marking up a feed, a calendar filling, a stamp landing. Three tiles unfold with the full content menu, shot list and caption starters, so detail is one click away instead of one long page.

Objection

The 20-minute planner

Drag four daily jobs into the quiet moments of a café day and an espresso bar fills to 20 minutes, then beans burst and a stamp lands. It answers “I don’t have time” by letting the owner try it, and ends beside the button. Drag, tap or keyboard.

Curriculum

Horizontal six weeks

The section pins and the weeks slide sideways with layered, parallax illustrations. Sideways reads as “a journey with an end”, and the end panel is the button.

Decision

Pricing with a compare toggle

Cards by default with the live cohort set apart; one toggle turns them into a side-by-side table for owners who want to check line by line. Magnetic buttons lean toward the pointer.

Everywhere

Coffee-ring cursor

A small latte dot that grows into a coffee ring and names the action over anything clickable: Reserve, Open, Drag, Compare. Mouse only. Touch devices keep their normal behaviour.

Phones

Sticky bar and smooth FAQ

On phones a bar with the price, time left and the button appears after the hero and hides over pricing and the closing band. FAQ answers ease open and close one at a time.

Guardrails

  • Reduced motion. With the system setting on, the page is a calm static version: headline already written, full feed shown, no pins, no tilt, no custom cursor, weeks in a swipeable row. Everything still works.
  • Keyboard. Visible focus on every control. The planner works with Tab and Enter, the tiles open with Enter, and the FAQ uses native disclosure elements.
  • Weight. No photos to download: every café image is drawn on canvas from a seed. Fonts and scripts are self-hosted. No tracking and no third-party requests.
  • Sound. The planner has small WebAudio clicks and a chime, off by default behind a labelled toggle.
  • Honest numbers. The phone story is labelled as an illustration, the countdown follows a real rule, and the results slot stays empty until there are real results.
  • Mobile. Checked at 390px wide: the button, price, date, refund and countdown sit in the first screen, with no sideways page scroll.

What to A/B test first

In priority order: biggest likely effect and cheapest to build first. One test at a time, with the success measure fixed before launch.

  1. Where the hero button goes

    Straight to checkout for the recommended tier, against scrolling to the three pricing cards.

    Measure: checkout starts per visitor, then completed orders. Guardrail: refund rate.

  2. Headline promise

    Effort-led (“in 20 minutes a day”) against outcome-led (“a month of posts scheduled in six weeks”).

    Measure: hero button clicks and scroll past the hero.

  3. Price in the hero

    $249 on the button and its own tile, against no price until pricing.

    Measure: completed orders, not clicks, since hiding the price can inflate clicks that never convert.

  4. Planner placement

    The planner straight after the bento grid, against after the FAQ, against no planner.

    Measure: checkout starts from the planner button and overall orders. Guardrail: scroll depth to pricing.

  5. Phone story length

    The pinned phone at its current scroll length, against half the length.

    Measure: scroll depth to the bento grid and pricing. Long pins can lose impatient visitors.

  6. Countdown

    Live ticking tile against a plain line with the closing date.

    Measure: orders and refund requests. A deadline that feels pushy can raise both.

A launch page rarely has the traffic for small tests. Until it does, test big differences like the first three, and pick the sample size before starting rather than stopping when a result looks good.

Recommended next

  • Collect real results from the first cohort (with permission) and fill the results slot.
  • Replace the illustrated instructor portrait with a real photo taken behind a counter.
  • Track five events: hero button click, checkout start, planner completed, FAQ question opened, sticky bar tap.
  • Add a short clip of one real lesson to the live-calls tile.

Build notes

  • Static HTML, CSS and two page scripts. GSAP with ScrollTrigger and Flip, and Lenis smooth scrolling, self-hosted in /vendor.
  • Self-hosted variable fonts: Fraunces for display, Caveat for chalk, Bricolage Grotesque for reading, DM Mono for labels and counters.
  • The hero headline is real text for screen readers, drawn as per-letter SVG paths for the chalk effect. Café posts are procedural canvas drawings.
