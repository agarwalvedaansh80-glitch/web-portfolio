Case study: one page, one job

Rebuilding the landing page for a 6-week course that teaches café owners to run their own Instagram. The goal was a single action: a visitor reserves a seat.

The problem

The original page looked tidy but never said what the course was, who it was for, what it cost or when it started. The only action was an eight-field enquiry form at the very bottom.

The approach

Answer the five questions a busy owner has (what, for me, how long, how much, what if it doesn't work) in the first screen, then handle each remaining doubt in the order it comes up.

The result

A page where the primary action is visible on the first screen at 1440px and 390px wide, the price is stated up front, and every section ends nearer a decision.

A note on honesty. The course, instructor and brand are fictional, and the page has had no traffic. There are no conversion numbers here and no invented testimonials on the page. Expected effects are described in plain terms, and the test plan below is how they would be proved.

The first screen, side by side

Most visitors decide whether to keep reading from what they see before scrolling, so this is where most of the work went.

What changed and why

Each change is tied to the doubt it removes. None of them is decoration.

Change and principleBeforeAfter

  • Headline

    Clear value proposition. Name the audience, the outcome and the effort in one line so the right visitor recognises themselves at once.

    Before

    "Unlock Your Digital Potential." True of any product.

    After

    "Run your café's Instagram in 20 minutes a day."

  • Primary action

    One goal, visible early. A single primary button that says what happens, repeated at each decision point and nowhere else.

    Before

    "Learn more" scrolls down; the real action is "Submit" under a form at the bottom.

    After

    "Reserve my seat" in the first screen, under pricing and in the closing band. Same words every time.

  • Price

    Cost transparency. A hidden price is a reason to leave and a reason to distrust. One recommended tier gives an easy default.

    Before

    "Pricing details are shared upon enquiry."

    After

    $249 in the hero; three tiers with the live cohort marked as recommended and set apart visually.

  • Form friction

    Fewer steps to commit. Ask only for what the next step needs.

    Before

    Eight fields, six required, including phone and employee count, to receive a quote.

    After

    No form on the page. The button goes straight to checkout, email first, no account.

  • Curriculum

    Outcomes over features. Owners buy what they'll have at the end of each week, not module titles.

    Before

    "Introduction to Social Media Ecosystems and Brand Identity Frameworks."

    After

    Six weeks in sequence, each ending in "You leave with…" a concrete deliverable.

  • Copy length

    Scannable text. Short blocks, specific nouns, plain verbs. People scan before they read.

    Before

    Four justified paragraphs of general claims in the first section.

    After

    Three one-line pain points in the owner's words, then a single sentence on how the course fixes them.

  • Who it's for, and not for

    Self-qualification. Saying who should not buy makes the rest of the page more believable and cuts refunds.

    Before

    "Businesses of all sizes."

    After

    Two short lists, including "you want thousands of followers fast".

  • Risk reversal

    Lower the cost of being wrong. A clear, simple refund makes the decision reversible.

    Before

    Nothing.

    After

    30-day refund stated in the hero and beside pricing, plus a free move to the next cohort.

  • Deadline

    Real, not manufactured, urgency. The countdown follows an actual rule (enrolment closes the Sunday before each monthly start) and never resets on reload.

    Before

    No dates at all.

    After

    Start date and time left to enrol in the hero, closing band and sticky bar.

  • Objections

    Answer doubts where they arise. Time, skill, camera shyness, results, missed calls, refunds.

    Before

    No FAQ. Questions meant a form and a wait.

    After

    Eight-question accordion, including an honest "we can't promise a number" answer.

  • Navigation

    Fewer exits. A landing page has one job; every extra link competes with it.

    Before

    Seven links, including Blog and Resources, plus four footer links.

    After

    Three in-page links and the primary button. Hidden on phones.

  • Mobile

    Design for the phone first. Owners browse between customers, one-handed.

    Before

    Desktop floats squeezed into 390px; nav wraps, the hero headline breaks one word per line.

    After

    Full-width button in the first screen and a sticky bar that appears after the hero and hides over pricing.

  • Social proof

    Only real proof counts. Generic claims ("proven strategies") read as filler; invented reviews are worse.

    Before

    "Expert instructors. Proven strategies. Real results."

    After

    A labelled slot reserved for real student results after the first cohort.

The interactive layer, and what each part is for

The first version was clear but read as plain. The second pass adds motion only where it shows the promise or lowers a doubt, and keeps every rule from the first pass: the button stays in the first screen, nothing moves for visitors who ask for less motion, and nothing invents proof.

  • Self-filling feed

    A phone beside the headline posts café illustrations one by one and tilts with the pointer. It shows the end state (a feed that keeps itself going) before a word is read.

  • Six-week scroll scene

    The curriculum pins while the phone, the active week and a sketch of weekly footfall move from week 1 to 6. The footfall line is labelled as a sketch, not data; week 6 is where owners learn to draw their own.

  • 20-minute planner

    Visitors drag four daily jobs into a café day and watch a ring fill to 20 minutes. It answers the biggest doubt in the FAQ ("is 20 minutes realistic?") by letting them try it. Works by drag, tap or keyboard, and ends next to the primary button.

  • Letterboard type

    The headline letters rise in once, then lift and thicken under the pointer using the variable weight axis. Letter boxes are fixed first, so the line never reflows.

  • Small things

    Magnetic primary buttons with a fill sweep, a cursor that names the action ("Reserve", "Drag", "Open"), a FAQ that eases open, price cards that lift and print an itemised receipt, and a sticky phone bar after the hero.

  • Guardrails

    No layout shift from scripts (the scene's scroll length is set in CSS before paint), the planner loads when it is near, the feed pauses off-screen, and with reduced motion the page is a calm, fully working static version.

What to A/B test first

In priority order: biggest likely effect and cheapest to build first. One test at a time, with the success measure fixed before launch.

  1. Where the hero button goes

    Straight to checkout for the recommended tier, against scrolling to the three-tier pricing table.

    Measure: checkout starts per visitor, then completed orders. Guardrail: refund rate.

  2. Headline promise

    Effort-led ("in 20 minutes a day") against outcome-led ("a month of posts scheduled in six weeks").

    Measure: hero button clicks and scroll past the hero.

  3. Price in the hero

    $249 on the letterboard against no price until the pricing section.

    Measure: completed orders, not clicks, since hiding the price can inflate clicks that never convert.

  4. Sticky mobile bar

    On against off, phones only.

    Measure: mobile checkout starts. Watch for accidental taps.

  5. Planner placement

    The 20-minute planner straight after the curriculum, against after the FAQ, against no planner.

    Measure: checkout starts from the planner button and overall orders. Guardrail: scroll depth to pricing.

  6. Countdown

    Live countdown against a plain line with the closing date.

    Measure: orders and refund requests. A deadline that feels pushy can raise both.

A launch page rarely has the traffic for small tests. Until it does, test big differences like the first three, and pick the sample size before starting rather than stopping when a result looks good.

Recommended next

  • Collect real results from the first cohort (with permission) and fill the results slot.
  • Replace the instructor placeholder with a real photo taken in a café.
  • Track four events: hero button click, checkout start, FAQ question opened, sticky bar tap. The FAQ data shows which doubts to answer higher up.
  • Add a short video of one real lesson beside the curriculum.

Build notes

  • Static HTML, CSS and one page script. GSAP with ScrollTrigger and Lenis smooth scrolling, self-hosted in /vendor. No framework, no tracking, no third-party requests.
  • Variable fonts self-hosted: Big Shoulders (weight 100 to 900) for the letterboard and week numbers, Instrument Sans for reading.
  • Visible keyboard focus, a keyboard path through the planner, reduced motion respected, layouts checked at 1440px and 390px. Illustrations are drawn in SVG: no stock photos, no brand logos.
  • No external research is cited here. The principles above are standard conversion practice; any claim about their effect on this page needs the tests above.
