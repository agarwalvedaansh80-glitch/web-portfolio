How to build a drive-time buffer that survives Monday

Most schedules break on the road, not on the job. Here is a simple way to size the gaps between visits so the afternoon still works.

MV
Marisol Vega
Dispatch lead, Slotted
Sep 30, 20264 min read
ON THIS PAGE

Ask a dispatcher where the day went wrong and the answer is rarely the job itself. A boiler service takes about as long as a boiler service takes. What breaks the afternoon is the forty minutes nobody planned for: the school run traffic, the van that needed fuel, the parking space three streets away.

A buffer is the time you deliberately leave empty between two visits. Done well, it absorbs the small delays so the customer at 3pm still gets the window you promised at 8am.

Start with real drive times, not distance

Two jobs that are four miles apart can be eight minutes or thirty-five minutes apart depending on the hour. Plan with the drive time for the hour the tech will actually be driving. If your tool only knows distance, add a fixed multiplier for rush hours and be honest about it.

Size the buffer to the job, not the day

A flat fifteen minutes everywhere wastes time after short, predictable visits and is too thin after diagnostic work. A better starting rule:

  • Routine maintenance: 10 minutes after the drive
  • Repairs and diagnostics: 20 minutes after the drive
  • Installs and first visits to a new site: 30 minutes after the drive

Review the numbers every two weeks. If a job type keeps overrunning, raise its buffer. If techs keep arriving early, lower it.

Protect Monday morning

Mondays carry the weekend backlog: emergency callbacks, parts that arrived on Friday, customers who waited two days with no heat. Leave one open slot per tech before 11am on Mondays. If nothing urgent comes in, pull a maintenance visit forward. If something does, you have somewhere to put it without moving four other customers.

A buffer you never use is not wasted. It is the reason the next customer did not have to call the office.

Make the buffer visible

Buffers fail when they are invisible, because the next person to look at the board sees an empty gap and fills it. Show buffers as their own blocks on the schedule, in a different colour, so everyone knows that space is spoken for.

On Slotted, drive time and buffers appear as dashed segments between jobs. When a job overruns, the buffer is consumed first, and only then does the rest of the day move.
